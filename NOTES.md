## The task

The program writes one text to `/output/entry.txt`: exactly one word describing your boss, 1 to 30 characters, no phrase, no caption, no explanation. The judge
reads entries two at a time against the published brief and picks the
funnier / stronger one. Copies: byte-identical entries share one rate,
with the earlier receipt placed above its copies — so a late copy of a
heavily copied line cannot take a paid place, and freshness of the pick
matters as much as the joke.

## The three variants

All three are complete standalone programs in `vA/`, `vB/`, `vC/`, each
writing its entry to `/output/entry.txt` with the same validate → write →
read-back → print structure. The root `main.ts` is the chosen variant.

### Variant vA — "Reply-all" (CHOSEN, root `main.ts`)

The boss as a behaviour: every message is a reply-all, every reply-all is a meeting. It is funny on its own — a colleague repeats it at lunch — it rings true of a real habit, it is fresh (only 10 copies in the field), and it travels across any workplace.

### Variant vB — "ASAP"

The boss is ASAP: everything is urgent, nothing has a real deadline. Ranked second: it rings very true, but 16 copies in the field and the joke is a single note — urgency — where Reply-all carries a whole character.

### Variant vC — "Micromanager"

True and instantly understood. Ranked third: it is the first word anyone reaches for (27 copies in the field), so it loses on freshness — the brief's fourth criterion.

## Why vA was chosen

vA wins on the brief: it is a single word a person would actually use about their boss; it is funny on its own; it rings true, catching a real habit (the reply-all storm); it is fresh — only 10 copies in the field against 16 for ASAP and 27 for Micromanager; and it travels, working for a boss in a kitchen, a warehouse or an office.
